I type at 63 words a minute. With Wispr Flow installed on my laptop and iPhone, I've averaged 110 over the past twelve months. Nearly double.

That's the hook. It isn't the point.

The point is that dictation has crossed a line in the last year or two. It used to be the thing you reached for when you couldn't type: accessibility, injury, holding a baby. Now it's a better default for a lot of knowledge work, and almost no one I work with has noticed. So this is me showing colleagues what's actually on the table. Not waving a personal best around.

The speed bit, briefly...

110 versus 63 is a 75% jump. If you write for a living, and in marketing you do, that compounds fast. A twenty-minute first draft becomes eleven. Across a week, hours back.

The obvious objection is that dictated text is messy. You ramble, you repeat yourself, the cleanup eats the gain. That used to be true. It's the bit people are out of date on.

Wispr isn't a transcriber. It edits as it goes. It cuts the umms, formats paragraphs and lists, and you can tell it how you want to sound: tone, formality, how heavy a hand it should take. It learns your custom spellings, the names of colleagues, product names, the acronyms in your industry, so it stops mangling them. And it carries all of that between your phone and your laptop.

So the fair comparison isn't messy 110 versus careful 63. The 110 arrives formatted, trimmed, spelled right. The cleanup is much smaller than you'd assume, and shrinking.

That alone would justify the switch. It's not why I kept going.

What changed...

When I type at 63, I edit as I go. I'm pruning sentences before they're finished. I keep things terse, partly because typing is effortful so brevity feels efficient, and partly because there's a quiet voice tidying everything in real time.

When I talk at 110, that voice can't keep up. I just say the whole thing. The messy version. The full context. The bit I'd normally cut for being too much.
That sounds like a downside. It isn't.

The tidy typed version is often tidy because I've quietly dropped the parts I wasn't sure how to phrase. Dictation surfaces them instead of burying them. And a loose, over-explained, context-rich brain dump is the wrong input for a human reader but the right input for an AI.
Dictation and AI

Hand Claude a tight, self-edited, 63-WPM prompt and you get a tight, slightly under-informed answer back. You've starved it of context without realising. The prompt was optimised for your effort, not the model's understanding.
Dictate the same request and you overshare. You give it the background, the constraint you'd have cut, the half-formed worry about the audience, the "oh and also" you'd never have bothered typing. The output is noticeably better. Not because the model changed. Because you finally told it what was in your head. And it's the same in my emails, or in my WhatsApp (yeah, I use it there too). I even wrote this blog with Wispr.

So the workflow that's emerged is stacked.

You dictate the full version. All the context, the bit you'd have cut, the "oh and also". The Wispr AI handles the structure. Brain dump in, draft out.
You provide judgement. Which is the only bit that was ever really yours to do.
Each layer removes work you used to do badly or slowly by hand.

Why most people are leaving this on the table...

Almost everyone I work with can type competently, so they do.

But I think they're missing something. The faster and more fluent a typist you are, the more aggressively you self-edit at the keyboard, and the more you have to gain from a tool that outruns that instinct. The people who'd benefit most are the ones most convinced they don't need it.

The barriers are real but smaller than they were. Talking to your machine feels strange, especially near other people. You get over that in about a week. You also have to stop treating the transcript as precious; it's input, not output, though with Wispr cleaning as it goes the gap is narrower than it used to be. And accuracy used to be the dealbreaker. It largely isn't. Once it's learned your names and product terms, the mangled-jargon problem disappears, and the learning carries between devices.

Takeaway

If this were a post about going from 63 to 110 words a minute, it'd be a productivity tip. Mildly interesting but generally ignored.

The real point is that dictation crossed a threshold while most of us weren't watching. It stopped being a transcriber and became something that formats, edits, shapes tone, learns your world. Paired with AI it changed what I produce, not just how fast.

The number got me to try it. But not why I'm telling you about it.

If you type for a living and you've filed dictation under "not for me, I'm already fast", then, yeah, I reckon it's for you. 

It is so much better than you think it is. And there's probably other tools just like it. I'm not paid by Wispr to say this - I just think it's excellent.
