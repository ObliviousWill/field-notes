When people start with AI they aim for something impressive: a strategy, a campaign, a clever app. It's understandable. But the work that has saved me the most time this year is the most boring work I do.

Two examples:

- **Every Monday at 7am**, an email lands with the three most important news stories from our industry that week. Each one says why it matters to us. ([More on that](/field-notes/projects/sector-news/).)
- **Every morning**, the key numbers for the business are gathered, checked and put on one page everyone can see. ([More on that](/field-notes/projects/kpi-reports/).)

Nobody has to remember to do either. They just happen.

## Why boring is the right place to start

Boring jobs have three things going for them:

1. **You already know exactly how to do them.** So you can write the instructions down, and you know a good result when you see one.
2. **They happen again and again.** Set it up once and it pays you back every week.
3. **Nobody misses doing them.** You won't meet any resistance from someone whose favourite task you've taken away.

## How to set one up

You don't need any special software for the news briefing. Claude can now run a task on a schedule, so it's a set of written instructions and a timer.

The instructions are where the work is. Write them as if you're briefing a new assistant on their first day:

- **What to look for.** "News from the last seven days about the UK education sector."
- **How to check it.** "Check the date on every story. Only use things from the last seven days."
- **What to produce.** "Three stories. For each: what happened, the link, why it matters to us, and an idea for sharing it with the team."
- **How to write it.** "Plain English. If a sentence wouldn't make sense to someone outside marketing, rewrite it."
- **Where to send it.** "Email it to these four people."

## The rules that stop it going wrong

A job that runs while you're asleep needs to be honest with you. Every one of mine has some version of these rules:

**Never make up a number.** If it can't get a figure, it says so. A report that is quietly wrong is worse than no report.

**Don't pad a quiet week.** If there are only two good stories, send two. A briefing that always finds three soon becomes noise.

**Zero isn't the same as missing.** In the school holidays, some of our numbers really are zero. If a question fails, the report keeps yesterday's figure and labels it, rather than showing a zero that looks real.

**Add it up two ways.** If the daily figures don't match the total, something is wrong. This has already caught a mistake.

**Tell a person.** It always sends one short message, and anything that needs a human comes first. A scheduled job that finds a problem and tells nobody has failed.

## Put it where people already look

My first news briefings came as an attached file. Nobody opened it. Now the whole briefing is in the body of the email, readable on a phone in two minutes.

The same thing happened with the numbers. The first version produced a PDF that only I could see. Now it updates one shared page that everyone has bookmarked.

The best output in the world doesn't help if it lands somewhere nobody looks.

## Try this

Think of one thing you do every week that involves gathering information and summing it up: a round-up, a report, a status update. Write down, step by step, how you do it. Then hand that to Claude and ask it to do the job on a timer.

Watch the first three runs closely. Then let it get on with it.
