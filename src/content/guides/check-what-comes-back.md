Claude writes well. That's useful, and it's also the trap. A polished answer *feels* right whether it's right or not.

Discernment, the third D, is the habit of judging what comes back instead of accepting it. You're the editor, not the audience.

## Three things to judge

**1. Is it right?** Are the facts true? Do the numbers add up? Did it answer the question you actually asked?

**2. Is it good?** Is it right for the person reading it? Is it too long? Does it sound like you? Is anything important missing?

**3. Did it get there sensibly?** If you asked it to explain its thinking, does the reasoning hold up? A right answer reached by a wrong route will let you down next time.

## Quick checks that catch most mistakes

- **Check anything you'll repeat.** Names, dates, figures, quotes, prices, laws. Look them up somewhere you trust.
- **Watch for things that are too neat.** A perfect statistic, a quote that says exactly what you wanted, a source you can't find. These are the classic signs it has filled a gap with something plausible.
- **Re-do the sums.** If there are totals or percentages, check two of them yourself.
- **Read it as the person receiving it.** Would your manager, your customer or your mum understand it, and would they mind how it's put?
- **Look for what's missing.** AI is good at answering what you asked and bad at noticing what you didn't.

## Make Claude check itself

You can turn discernment into part of the conversation:

- "How confident are you in this? What are you least sure about?"
- "What would someone who disagreed with this say?"
- "Check your answer against my original request. What did you miss?"
- "List every fact in this that I should double-check."

It's surprisingly honest when asked. It won't catch everything, but it will catch a lot.

## Know when to stop

Sometimes the best judgement is to drop it and start again. If you've corrected the same thing three times, the conversation has gone off track. Start a fresh chat with a better brief (that's Description, from guides 3 and 4).

## Try this today

Take the last useful thing Claude wrote for you. Ask it: "List every fact in this that I should double-check." Then check two of them.
